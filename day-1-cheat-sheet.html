Chapter 1

Tasting and evaluating wine

The WSET Systematic Approach to Tasting (SAT): appearance, nose, palate, then a quality conclusion.

Must know

  1. Most common colours: red = ruby; white = lemon. A red with some age = garnet.
  2. Oak and malolactic conversion aromas are secondary aromas.
  3. Cork taint smells of damp cardboard.
  4. Balance = how flavour, sweetness, tannins and acidity work together.

The SAT scales

Appearance
Intensity: pale – medium – deep. White: lemon – gold – amber. Rosé: pink – pink-orange – orange. Red: purple – ruby – garnet – tawny.
Nose
Intensity: light – medium – pronounced. Aroma characteristics: primary, secondary, tertiary.
Palate
Sweetness: dry – off-dry – medium – sweet. Acidity, tannin, alcohol: low – medium – high. Body: light – medium – full. Flavour intensity: light – medium – pronounced. Finish: short – medium – long.
Conclusion
Quality: poor – acceptable – good – very good – outstanding.

Judging quality: four criteria

The four
Balance (sugar balanced by acid; alcohol and acid balanced by fruit) · Length of the finish · Intensity and identifiable flavours · Complexity.
Scoring
All four positive = outstanding · three = very good · two = good · one = acceptable · none = poor. An unbalanced wine is rarely more than acceptable.
Tips
  • Judge acidity by how much and how long your mouth waters; sugar can mask acid, and alcohol burns like acid.
  • Body comes mostly from alcohol; sugar adds body, high acid makes it feel lighter, ripe tannin fuller.
  • Tannins dry the mouth and can taste bitter; they come mostly from the skins.
  • Only count pleasant sensations in the finish.
Setup
Good light, no strong smells, clean palate, no perfume. ISO glass (rounded bowl, inward-sloping walls); pour about 5 cL.

Chapter 2

Pairing wine and food

Food usually changes the wine more than the wine changes the food, and more often for the worse.

Must know

  1. Sweetness and umami make wine "harder": more drying, bitter and acidic, less sweet and fruity.
  2. Salt and acid make wine "softer": less drying and acidic, sweeter and more fruity.
  3. Match flavour intensity: food and wine should be equally intense.
  4. Chilli heat is worst with high-alcohol wines.

Chapter 3

Storage and service of wine

Everything here is about serving the wine in the best possible condition.

Must know

  1. Poor storage can create faulted wines. Store cool, at a constant temperature, away from bright light; cork-sealed bottles on their side.
  2. Room temperature = 18°C.
  3. Sparkling goes in a flute (or tulip glass). Vintage Port should be decanted.
  4. A blanket system adds a layer of gas to protect the leftover wine from oxidation.

Chapter 4

Grape-growing and labelling terms

A vine can live for over 60 years. It needs warmth, sunlight, carbon dioxide, water and nutrients.

Must know

  1. Order: flowering → fruit set → véraison → ripening. At véraison grapes change colour: white to golden, black to red then purple.
  2. The pulp holds sugars and acids (and water, flavour). Skins hold flavour, colour and tannin; seeds and stems hold bitter tannins.
  3. Most vineyards lie 30–50° north and south of the Equator.
  4. Picked later = higher sugar, lower acid. Cooler than usual vintages give too much acid.
  5. Grenache does well in warm climates but not cool ones. Machine harvesting works for wines of any quality (not on steep slopes).
  6. Fungal diseases damage vine leaves and spoil grape quality.

Chapter 5

Winemaking

Yeast turn grape sugar into alcohol, CO₂ and heat. Dry wines are usually 11.5–16% alcohol.

Must know

  1. White order: crushing → pressing → fermentation → maturation.
  2. Red order: fermentation → draining → pressing → maturation (crushing comes first).
  3. Fermentation temperatures: white 12–22°C; red 20–32°C.
  4. Red colour extraction: pumping over or punching down the cap of skins.
  5. Oak gives vanilla and toast. Lees give biscuit and bread.

Winemaking options

Adjustments
Add sugar in cool climates (more alcohol, body); add acid in warm climates; remove acid in cool climates.
Inert vessels
Stainless steel or concrete: add no flavour, can be airtight.
Oak
New oak: vanilla, coconut, charred wood, spice; toasting adds sweet spice. Old oak adds little. Oxygen through the wood gives caramel, dried fruit, nuts and softens tannin, faster in small barrels. Chips and staves are cheap alternatives.
Malolactic
Bacteria lower acidity and can add butter. Nearly always in reds; optional in whites, usually avoided for aromatic grapes like Riesling.
Lees
Stirring dead yeast through the wine adds body, biscuit and bread; usually avoided for aromatic grapes.
Blending
For consistency year to year, or for complexity.

White · cool, moderate and warm

Chardonnay

The copycat. Climate sets the fruit and body; winemaking adds the rest. Always dry.

Must know

  1. Chardonnay is not always oaked. Winemaking options: malolactic conversion (butter, cream; softens acid), lees stirring (more body, bread, biscuit), oak (smoke, vanilla, coconut).
  2. Chablis = lemon, apple and wet stones, almost never oaky.
  3. Pouilly-Fuissé = full-bodied, with tropical fruit and oak.
  4. Very good examples age for years or decades: hazelnut, mushroom.

White · cool to moderate · aromatic

Riesling

The rigid aristocrat. Razor acid, never oaked, any sweetness level.

Must know

  1. Aromatic, high acid, unoaked (inert vessels keep the floral aromas). Dry to sweet, light to full body.
  2. Flavour follows ripeness. Just-ripe: apple, lime, lemon (also pear). Extra-ripe: peach, apricot, mango, pineapple, sometimes dried apricot and raisin.
  3. Sweet styles come from late harvest, botrytis/noble rot, or grapes freezing on the vine. High acid balances the sugar.
  4. Ages for decades: honey and petrol.
  5. German Prädikat levels are set by sugar levels at harvest. Least to most ripe: Kabinett → Spätlese → Auslese → Beerenauslese.

White · cool to moderate · aromatic

Sauvignon Blanc

The twitchy gardener. Herbaceous, zippy, best young.

Must know

  1. Typical aromas: grass and citrus. Herbaceous (grass, green bell pepper, asparagus) and floral (blossom).
  2. Typically dry, high acid, light to medium body, unoaked, single varietal. Drink young: even top wines lose their fresh aromas within a couple of years.
  3. Loire AOCs: Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé. Pouilly-Fuissé is Chardonnay.
  4. In Pessac-Léognan it is blended with Sémillon, which adds body and ageing potential.

White · dry to sweet

Chenin Blanc, Sémillon and Furmint

The sweet-wine trio. All susceptible to botrytis/noble rot, all made dry to sweet, all able to age.

Must know

  1. Vouvray AOC = Chenin Blanc. Chenin is South Africa's most widely planted white grape.
  2. Sauternes = Sémillon, concentrated by botrytis. Serve well chilled (6–8°C).
  3. Tokaji Aszú = Furmint, from Tokaj in Hungary. Always sweet; 6 Puttonyos is sweeter than 5.
  4. Spelling: Sémillon in France, Semillon in English-speaking countries.

Red · cool to moderate

Pinot Noir

The fragile artist. Thin-skinned, pale to medium colour, sulks in heat.

Must know

  1. High acid, low to medium tannin, strawberry, raspberry, red cherry.
  2. Too warm and it loses its fresh fruit and tastes of cooked fruit. Prone to rot and disease.
  3. Can be enjoyed young. Very good examples age into mushroom and forest floor.
  4. Burgundy Grand Cru must come from a single vineyard. Gevrey-Chambertin and Nuits-Saint-Georges are famous Pinot villages.
  5. Typically single varietal (except sparkling, e.g. Champagne). Careful with oak: older or larger barrels; the best show subtle smoke and cloves.

Red · moderate to warm · Bordeaux

Cabernet Sauvignon vs Merlot

Bordeaux's blending partners. Cabernet is the stern, thick-skinned one; Merlot the soft, medium one.

Must know

  1. Left Bank (Médoc, Haut-Médoc with Margaux and Pauillac, Graves with Pessac-Léognan) = Cabernet-led. Right Bank (Saint-Émilion, Pomerol) = Merlot-led. St-Émilion Grand Cru is mostly Merlot; Pomerol is Right Bank.
  2. Why blend: Merlot lowers tannin and acid, makes wine ready sooner and adds red fruit. Cabernet adds tannin and acidity.
  3. Cabernet's high acid and tannin let it age a long time. Gravel soils help it ripen on the Left Bank: they drain rain and store heat.
  4. Labels: Cru Bourgeois = good Médoc wine outside the Grand Cru Classé system. Château = estate-grown grapes. Bordeaux Supérieur has slightly stricter rules than Bordeaux AOC.

Red · moderate and warm only · same grape, two styles

Syrah vs Shiraz

The peppery Frenchman with a loud Australian alter ego. Thick skin, small grapes, medium to high tannin and acid.

Must know

  1. Typical profile: blackberry, black pepper, herbal.
  2. Classic region: Northern Rhône. Best AOCs: Côte-Rôtie and Hermitage. Hand-harvested because the vineyards are on steep slopes.
  3. Blended with Grenache to add colour and tannin.
  4. Barossa Valley has some of the oldest vines: full body, ripe tannins, cooked black fruit.

France → Northern Rhône: moderate; steep slopes, sunlight reflected off the river; the only black grape allowed.
→ Côte-Rôtie AOC: Syrah's northern limit; complex, peppery; sometimes co-fermented with a little Viognier for floral notes.
→ Hermitage AOC: one steep south-facing hill.
→ Crozes-Hermitage AOC: flatter, larger; less intense and cheaper.
France → South of France: blended into Minervois AOC; single varietal as Pays d'Oc IGP.
Australia: most planted grape. South Eastern Australia for high volume. Hunter Valley: medium body, medium to high tannin, fresh black fruit; earth and meat with age.
With age
A decade or more for the best: dried fruit, leather, meat, earth.

Grenache / Garnacha

The jammy sunbather. Garnacha in Spain, where it started; Grenache in France.

Must know

  1. Needs a lot of heat to ripen fully: warm climates only.
  2. High sugar, thin skins, low acidity; low to medium tannin.
  3. Strawberry, raspberry, white pepper; also red plum, red cherry, liquorice.
  4. Often blended with Syrah and Tempranillo. Often used for rosé.
  5. Côtes du Rhône reds are mostly Grenache; Châteauneuf-du-Pape is a Grenache-based Southern Rhône cru; Priorat DOCa makes Spain's most powerful Garnacha.

Winemaking
Best wines need controlled yields. Simple wines unoaked; top wines barrel-matured, often in old oak. Rosé: short maceration, dry to medium, drink young and chilled.
Regions
France → Southern Rhône: warm, flat valley; blends with Syrah and other local grapes. Châteauneuf-du-Pape AOC: full body, high alcohol; large round stones store heat.
France → South of France → Minervois AOC: with Syrah.

Australia → Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale: many old vines; also blended with Shiraz.
