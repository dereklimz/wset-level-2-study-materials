White · cool to moderate · same grape, two styles

Pinot Gris vs Pinot Grigio

The twins. Pinot Grigio is the Italian name, Pinot Gris the French one.

Must know

  1. The style difference comes mostly from grape-growing and harvesting choices. Grigio: high yields, picked early while acidity is high. Gris: controlled yields, picked later and riper. Later-harvested grapes have higher sugars and lower acidity.
  2. Pinot Grigio delle Venezie DOC is simple and inexpensive.
  3. Vendanges Tardives means grapes picked later in the harvest season. Vendanges Tardives Pinot Gris is medium to sweet.
  4. On labels outside Italy and France, "Pinot Grigio" signals the high-acid, light style and "Pinot Gris" the fuller, more complex one.

Labels · Italy

Italian label terms

Italian wines are named after their region (Barolo, Valpolicella) or after grape plus region (Barbera d'Asti = Barbera from Asti).

Must know

  1. PDO (Protected Designation of Origin): small areas, tightly defined rules, many of Europe's most famous wines. Italy's PDO terms are DOCG and DOC.
  2. PGI (Protected Geographical Indication): much wider areas, less strict rules, more freedom over grapes and winemaking. Italy's PGI term is IGT.
  3. Classico = from the historic centre of a region, often the hilliest part, usually more concentrated.
  4. Riserva = a DOC or DOCG wine aged for at least a set number of months before release.

White · northern Italy

Garganega (Soave) vs Cortese (Gavi)

Two high-acid, unoaked whites known by their place names.

Must know

  1. Soave DOC and Soave Classico DOC = Garganega (Veneto). Gavi DOCG = Cortese (Piemonte).
  2. Both are fermented in inert vessels and not matured in oak.
  3. Gavi is ready to drink on release and not intended to age.
  4. Recioto di Soave DOCG is a sweet white made from dried grapes (appassimento).

White · central and southern Italy

Verdicchio vs Fiano

Verdicchio is the zippy, green-fruited one; Fiano is the riper, peachy one.

Must know

  1. Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi DOC is the most famous Verdicchio wine (Marche, central Italy's east coast).
  2. Most Verdicchio is simple, fruity and not intended for ageing. Outstanding examples develop honey and nuts.
  3. Fiano di Avellino DOCG comes from mountainous Campania in southern Italy. Usually drunk young; very good examples develop honey.

Red · moderate · Piemonte

Nebbiolo vs Barbera

Neighbours in north-west Italy. Both high acid; Nebbiolo has high tannin, Barbera has little.

Must know

  1. Barolo DOCG = 100% Nebbiolo. Barbaresco DOCG (smaller, north-east of Barolo) is also Nebbiolo. Both are powerful and long-lived.
  2. Barbera d'Asti DOCG makes what many consider Barbera's best wines.
  3. Nebbiolo often improves with age thanks to its high acid and tannin: mushroom, tobacco, leather.
  4. Barbera is enjoyable younger than Nebbiolo and has less ageing potential.

Red · warm · central Italy

Sangiovese vs Montepulciano

Central Italy's two big reds, split by the Apennines: Sangiovese in Tuscany to the west, Montepulciano in Abruzzo to the east.

Must know

  1. Sangiovese = Chianti DOCG, Chianti Classico DOCG, Brunello di Montalcino DOCG. Montepulciano d'Abruzzo is not Sangiovese.
  2. Brunello di Montalcino is a full-bodied, ageworthy red with very high tannin and acid. Brunello is the local name for Sangiovese.
  3. Montepulciano d'Abruzzo DOC is a high-volume, inexpensive red.
  4. Chianti Classico Riserva DOCG has stricter ageing rules and may show some tertiary notes on release.

Red · moderate · Veneto

Corvina

The raisin dryer. High acid, low to medium tannin, red cherry and red plum. Fresh grapes make Valpolicella; dried grapes make Amarone and Recioto.

Must know

  1. Corvina is the main grape of a Valpolicella blend; other local grapes are allowed.
  2. Appassimento = picking grapes and drying them indoors to concentrate sugars, acids, tannins and flavours.
  3. Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG and Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG are the two appassimento wines.

Red and rosé · warm · California and Italy

Zinfandel vs Primitivo

Arnold Schwarzenegger with two passports. Same grape: Zinfandel in California, Primitivo in Puglia.

Must know

  1. Needs a warm climate. Ripens unevenly: some berries raisin before others ripen, giving high sugar and dried-fruit flavours (prune, raisin).
  2. As it ripens, flavours move from red fruit (strawberry, raspberry) to black fruit (plum, black cherry).
  3. Premium Californian red Zinfandel = blackberry, prune, vanilla. Dry, medium to high acid and tannin, high alcohol, full body.
  4. White Zinfandel is a medium-sweet rosé: low alcohol (8–10%), not oaked, simple strawberry and raspberry.

White · aromatic · full-bodied

Gewurztraminer vs Viognier

Two of the world's fullest-bodied, most floral whites: Gewurztraminer is the perfume aunt, Viognier the sun goddess.

Must know

  1. Gewurztraminer's key aromas: rose and lychee.
  2. Alsace Gewurztraminer = low acidity, full-bodied, high alcohol. Its high sugar ferments to high alcohol.
  3. Condrieu AOC (Northern Rhône) is home to the finest Viognier; it is single-varietal Viognier only.
  4. Both are aromatic, with low acid and high alcohol.

White · moderate · Spain

Albariño

The Spanish coastal white. Zippy, lemon and peach.

Must know

  2. Dry, high acid, medium-bodied, single varietal.

Red · moderate · France

Gamay

The party cousin. Light, juicy, red-fruited, made for early drinking.

Must know

  1. High acidity, low to medium tannin, red cherry and raspberry (also red plum).
  2. Beaujolais is the famous Gamay region, directly south of Burgundy.
  3. Typically light-bodied and unoaked, usually made in inert vessels.
  4. Banana and candy aromas come from techniques that maximise colour but minimise tannin.

Red · moderate to warm · Spain

Tempranillo and Spanish labels

The timekeeper. Spain's most prized black grape, best known for its ageing ladder.

Must know

  1. Medium acidity, medium tannin, red cherry and blackberry (also strawberry and black plum).
  2. Rioja DOCa is the most famous region. Ribera del Duero DO makes very full-bodied Tempranillo with dark fruit.
  3. Ageing ladder, shortest to longest: Joven → Crianza → Reserva → Gran Reserva. These are national Spanish terms.
  4. Good Tempranillo ages into mushroom, dried fruit and leather.
  5. Spanish labels: PDO = DO and DOCa. PGI = Vino de la Tierra.

Red · warm · South America

Malbec vs Carmenère

Two French-born grapes that found fame in South America. Both deeply coloured, full-bodied, high tannin, often oaked, blended or single varietal.

Must know

  1. Malbec is Argentina's most important black grape; most plantings are in high-altitude Mendoza. Native to south-west France.
  2. Carmenère is native to Bordeaux (rarely found there now) and widely grown in Chile.
  3. Carmenère needs a long, sunny season; even in Chile it struggles outside the warmest Central Valley sites.
  4. Unripe Carmenère = pungent green bell pepper and tomato leaf.

South Africa's own red, bred for its warm, dry climate. Closely related to Pinot Noir.

Sparkling · bottle or tank fermentation

Sparkling wines

The bubbles are CO₂ from a second fermentation, trapped in a sealed bottle or a sealed tank. The base wine is dry, high in acid, low in alcohol and usually a blend.

Must know

  1. Traditional method, in order: autolysis → riddling → disgorging → dosage. Used for Champagne, Cava and Méthode Cap Classique.
  2. Autolysis (the lees breaking down) gives bread and biscuit notes. The longer on the lees, the more intense.
  3. Non-vintage Champagne is a blend of different years. Vintage Champagne comes from one exceptional year, and gains nuts and honey from bottle age.
  4. Tank method: Prosecco DOC (Glera). Asti DOCG is sweet and fruity, made from Muscat (Moscato). Cava is not a tank-method wine.
  5. Brut = dry. Dosage sets the final sweetness; Demi-Sec is medium sweet.

Traditional method
  1. Base wine + yeast + sugar go into a bottle sealed with a crown cap.
  2. Second fermentation in the bottle adds CO₂ and about 1.5% alcohol.
  3. Yeast autolysis: the lees break down, adding bread and biscuit.
  4. Riddling: the lees slide into the bottle neck (by hand, or by gyropalette).
  5. Disgorgement: the neck is frozen and the plug of ice and lees is forced out.
  6. Dosage: topped up with liqueur d'expédition (wine and, usually, sugar).
  7. Resealed with a thick cork and wire cage.
Tank method
Base wine, yeast and sugar ferment in a sealed tank; lees filtered out; bottled under pressure. Tastes of the base wine, with no autolytic notes. Cheaper, and suits aromatic grapes.
Asti method
Starts from grape juice. CO₂ escapes at first, then the tank is sealed to keep it. Fermentation is stopped by filtering out the yeasts, leaving a sweet, low-alcohol wine.
Labels
Traditional Method / Méthode traditionnelle = bottle fermented. Méthode Cap Classique = South Africa's traditional method (Champagne grapes or others, such as Chenin Blanc). Champagne and Cava don't have to state their PDO on the label. Vintage sparkling wines usually cost more than non-vintage ones from the same producer.
New World
South Africa, California, Australia and New Zealand: traditional method, best from the coolest regions, often Champagne grapes, slightly riper fruit than Champagne.
Service
Well chilled, 6–10°C, in a flute or tulip glass. Opening: hold the cork secure while loosening the cage, hold the bottle at an angle, and turn the bottle, not the cork.

Fortified · southern Spain

Sherry

Fortified after fermentation, so the starting point is dry. Most Sherry is dry; some is sweetened later.

Must know

  1. The base wine is a simple dry white, made from Palomino, around Jerez de la Frontera.
  2. Fortification happens after fermentation.
  3. The solera system is how Sherry ages: old-oak casks of wines of different ages, continually blended for a consistent, complex style.
  4. Fino's bready flavour comes from flor, a layer of yeast (biological ageing). Oloroso is aged oxidatively, without flor.

Fortified · Portugal · always sweet

Port

Fortified during fermentation, so it stays sweet and strong. Made in the Upper Douro from local black grapes; most Ports blend several vintages.

Must know

  1. Adding grape spirit stops the fermentation by killing the yeast, leaving a sweet, high-alcohol wine.
  2. Vintage Port is sweet, complex and concentrated. With long bottle age its colour turns from ruby to garnet.
  3. Vintage Port should be decanted; it forms a thick sediment.
  4. An age indication of 10, 20, 30 or 40 years means Tawny Port.
